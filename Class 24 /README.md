# Causal ML — Double Machine Learning for 401(k) Policy Evaluation

## Objective

Apply Double Machine Learning (DML) to estimate the causal effect of 401(k) eligibility on household net financial assets, demonstrating how orthogonalization resolves the regularization bias that contaminates naive ML-based causal estimation and enabling flexible high-dimensional confounder control while preserving valid inference.

## Methodology

- Demonstrated regularization bias through a simulated data-generating process with a known true ATE of 5.0, showing that a naive LASSO regression shrinks the treatment coefficient to 4.94 (−1.2% bias) because the L1 penalty fails to distinguish the causal parameter from nuisance covariates during variable selection.
- Loaded the Chernozhukov-Hansen 401(k) dataset (n=9,915) and constructed a `DoubleMLData` object with net total financial assets (`net_tfa`) as the outcome, 401(k) eligibility (`e401`) as the treatment, and 12 observed confounders spanning demographic (age, family size, marital status, education), economic (income, total wealth, non-401(k) financial assets, home ownership), and retirement-related (defined benefit coverage, IRA participation, 401(k) participation, dual-earner status) dimensions.
- Specified Random Forest regressors (`n_estimators=200`, `max_depth=5`) as nuisance learners for both the outcome equation (E[Y|X]) and the treatment equation (E[D|X]), chosen for their ability to capture nonlinearities and interactions without manual feature engineering while remaining robust to overfitting.
- Estimated the pooled ATE using a DoubleML Partially Linear Regression (PLR) model with 5-fold cross-fitting, which breaks the dependence between nuisance estimates and treatment effect residuals to preserve asymptotic normality and valid standard errors under high-dimensional confounding.
- Extended the analysis to Conditional Average Treatment Effects (CATE) by partitioning the sample into income quartiles and fitting separate DML models within each subgroup, allowing the nuisance functions to learn group-specific confounding structures rather than imposing a single functional form across the income distribution.
- Conducted sensitivity analysis via `dml_plr.sensitivity_analysis()` with partial-R² bounds of 3% on both the outcome and treatment equations to quantify how robust the pooled ATE is to a plausible unmeasured confounder such as financial literacy or risk aversion.

## Key Findings

- The naive LASSO benchmark confirmed textbook regularization bias: with 21 of 101 coefficients retained, the treatment estimate was attenuated toward zero (4.94 vs true 5.0), illustrating why ML methods cannot be applied directly to causal estimation without an orthogonalization framework.
- DML produced a pooled ATE of −\$867 (SE \$481, 95% CI [−\$1,810, \$76], p=0.072), which is not statistically significant at the 5% level and runs opposite in sign to the canonical Poterba-Venti-Wise result, suggesting that once high-dimensional confounding is controlled for flexibly, the apparent savings-boosting effect of 401(k) eligibility may reflect substitution between savings vehicles rather than new saving.
- CATE analysis revealed substantial heterogeneity across income quartiles, with point estimates ranging from +\$384 in Q1 (lowest income) to −\$1,658 in Q4 (highest income), though all four 95% confidence intervals crossed zero and no pairwise difference reached statistical significance given the reduced subsample sizes (~2,480 per quartile).
- Only Q1 showed a positive point estimate, suggesting that any causal effect of 401(k) eligibility is concentrated among low-income households who have fewer existing savings vehicles to substitute from, while middle and high-income households appear to reshuffle existing balances rather than increase total net worth.
- Sensitivity analysis produced a robustness value of 1.48% and an adjusted robustness value of 0.13%, indicating the pooled estimate is highly fragile to unmeasured confounding: a hypothetical omitted variable explaining just 1.48% of residual variation in both outcome and treatment would be sufficient to move the point estimate to zero, which is a low bar given that plausible confounders like financial literacy, risk aversion, and time preferences are absent from the dataset.
- At the benchmark 3% confounding assumption, the bounds on the true ATE widened to [−\$2,643, +\$909] for the point estimate and [−\$3,457, +\$1,693] for the 95% confidence interval, which spans both economically meaningful negative and positive effects and provides essentially no information about the sign of the true treatment effect.
- The combined results illustrate a critical methodological lesson: DML's flexibility in handling high-dimensional observed confounders does not rescue identification when the conditional independence assumption is in doubt, and sensitivity analysis should be treated as an essential honesty check rather than an optional robustness exercise when communicating causal estimates to policymakers.
- From a policy standpoint, the efficiency-equity tradeoff tilts toward targeting low-income households (Q1) where the effect is at least directionally positive and where households have the greatest unmet retirement savings need, while acknowledging that the wide confidence intervals across all quartiles preclude strong conclusions about where a program would be most impactful.
